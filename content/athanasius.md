## For the spiritually nervous

The idea of rejecting penal substitution can feel like abandoning your faith. I
assure you that it doesn't have to be that way. So if you're spiritually
sensitive, but worried about deconstructing into agnosticism or atheism, I have
a bit of practical advice to help.

Start by reading Athanasius' *On the Incarnation*. Understanding the ontological
atonement theory is the soft landing that is needed for people who have only
ever heard the gospel through the penal substitution frame. If you're here
because penal substitution has brought you to your end, I think you'll find
Athanasius to be a breath of fresh air.

My goal with this project has never been to tear down your faith, but rather to
strengthen it. Indeed, I built this whole thing so that I could hold onto mine.

## Why this book in particular

Penal substitution runs as follows:

> Sin → Guilt → Necessary Punishment → Death

So the question it forces you to ask is "how can we have our guilt forgiven so
that the necessary punishment does not fall on our shoulders?"

Athanasius (mostly) runs as follows:

> Sin → Corruption → Death

This restructure removes God from the sin-to-death pipeline. God has no part in
our movement towards death, which means he has only one role in salvation:
restoring.

Christ, through the incarnation, by joining himself to sinful man, has assumed
humanity, and through it heals it from the inside. His death on the cross is not
him receiving punishment from God so that God would be satisfied, but rather is
the work of condemning sin in the flesh. By not retaliating, Christ refused to
recycle sinfulness, which would cause corruption in his humanity, and for this
reason he is incorruptible. The healing nature of Christ overflows to all of
humanity and can be seen clearly in his miracles. By having faith in him, we can
partake of the divine qualities of God and we will also be healed.

God is both the doctor and the antidote. We are healed by going to him.
