*The argument, told sideways. If you want it straight — the tables, the
citations, the seventeen-century migration — read [the essay](/necessity-problem/).*

## I

Before there were wizards there was a physician.

He came in the plague years, when the world was going grey at the edges, and he did not come with instruments or bargains. He came in and lay down among the dying, and the sickness that had gone unanswered for so long found itself, for the first time, answered — not paid off, not argued with, but *outlasted*.

He said one thing before he left, and he said it kindly: *Death follows a turning-away. I have told you so, and I do not unsay what I say.*

He meant it as a physician means a diagnosis. He kept his word because he loved them, and the only chain he wore was one he had wound around his own wrist, and he wore it lightly, and it was not heavy, and there was no monster anywhere in the world.

## II

The wizards came later, and the first thing they found was the Beast.

It lived in the dark under the world and it held the deeds to men's souls in a fist that had never once opened. It was hungry — hungry in the way winter is cold, hungry as a *fact* — and the earliest of the wizards looked at it and thought: *very well. It must be fed, and then it will let go.*

So they carried the price down into the dark and set it before the mouth, and the mouth took it, and the fist opened, and men walked out into the light.

And the wizards were not troubled, because the Beast was *outside*. Out under the roots, in the cold, where a hungry thing belongs. The House on the hill was clean, and its Lord had no appetite at all.

## III

Anselm, first of the great wizards, could not bear it.

"Look how we live," he said. "We go down into the dark and we treat with a thing that has no right to anything. We *bargain*. Every year we bargain. It has no claim on us. It has no claim on *him*." And he was right, and the House had known he was right for a long time without saying so.

So he went down with a lamp and a staff and he broke the Beast's claim. Not its neck — the wizards would learn, too late, that a claim and a life are different things — but its *standing*. He unmade its title. He drove it out of the story, and the roots were empty, and the men of that age wept for joy in the streets.

He came back up the hill in the evening. He climbed the steps of the House. He crossed the great hall.

And something came in behind him, on the hem of his cloak, low to the floor, small as a cat.

They did not see it for a hundred years. What they saw was that the House had grown a new rule: that the Lord's *honour* must be answered before any man could be forgiven. It seemed a noble rule. It seemed like dignity. Nobody noticed that a rule which must be answered is only ever a mouth with better manners.

And a stranger thing, which no wizard said aloud: it had begun to matter what men did. A sin in a village could ring a bell in the House's kitchens. Something up there could be made to *want*.

## IV

Calvin came four centuries on and found the House uneasy.

"Honour can be waived," he said. "A great lord may let an insult pass and be greater for it. If honour is all that stands between us and mercy, then nothing stands there, and everything we have built stands on nothing." And that was true too. It was always true. Every move in this story is made by someone who is right.

So Calvin traded honour for *justice*, and a gift for a *debt*, and this was the hinge on which the age turned — because a gift may come from any hand, but a debt has a name written on it, and it will not be satisfied by a stranger.

Which meant he needed a cart.

He built it that winter: a low black cart to carry a name across the floor of the House and set it down in front of someone else. It ran well. It ran both ways. It is still running.

And the thing on the floor had grown to the size of a hound, and it followed the cart everywhere, and it had begun to be *fed*.

## V

Then a traveller came to the gate, out of the east, in the year of the long rain, and he would not come in.

He stood in the road and called up to the windows, and what he called was this:

"*If your Lord could have forgiven — why the blood? And if the blood paid all — what was forgiven?*"

The wizards came to the windows one by one and no one answered him. He waited three days in the rain and then he went away, and every wizard who ever lived after that was answering him, whether they said his name or not.

## VI

Owen was the greatest of them, and it is important that you understand he was not a fool and not a villain. He was the best mind the House ever raised, and he gave it everything he had.

His first answer was a ledger. *No debt is paid twice.* Elegant, precise, and built on a rule that only ever made sense while there was a captor in the dark who let go on payment — and Anselm had driven the captor out five hundred years before. Owen was turning a key in a door that was no longer standing. The hound watched him do it and grew.

So he tried again, and this is the moment the story turns.

He went down into the under-floors of the House with a lantern, past the cellars, past the foundation stones, down to where the bedrock is, and he found the thing waiting for him there. It was not a hound any more. It filled the dark from wall to wall, and it had the patience of something that has never been in danger.

And Owen looked at it and understood, with terrible clarity, the only thing that could make the whole edifice hold:

"You are not a rule," he said. "You are not a debt or a decree or a thing he chose. You are *what he is*. He can no more pass over sin than cease to be himself."

And he set it into the foundation stone, and mortared it in, and the House stopped shifting for the first time in a thousand years, and every wizard above him felt the floors go steady and did not know why.

He had made the cross necessary. He had also put a mouth in the bedrock of the world.

And he was a careful enough man to see, on the walk back up, what he had done — because a mouth in the foundations of *eternity* is a mouth that stood empty and starving through all the ages before there was any man to sin, and found its first meal only when we made one. It had been waiting. It had *always* been waiting. And we had brought it its supper.

Two years he sat with that. Then he went back down, because a mouth that must be filled to the brim leaves no room in the House for mercy and no leave to accept a substitute — and he cut a door in it. *That it eats is fixed. Who it eats is free.*

That is the door the Lamb walked through. Understand that Owen meant it as mercy. It was the only mercy left in the design.

## VII

Turretin came after and made the finest cut in the history of the craft.

Not between what the Lord *is* and what the Lord *chooses* — the seam Owen had used — but *inside the thing itself.* The mouth, he said, is always open. The eating is free. Necessity in the appetite, liberty in the meal.

It was beautiful work. Every wizard since has admired it.

And when he had finished, the thing in the foundations had a name and a shape and a place in the architecture, and could no longer be mistaken for anything else, and was no smaller than before. He had not caged it. He had *labelled* it. The House was quieter after Turretin, the way a room is quiet when everyone in it has agreed not to mention something.

## VIII

There were four hundred years of small work after that. Knots tied, circuits closed, the cart improved. Nobody moved the thing again. Every wizard who went down to look at it came back up saying it was necessary, and each of them said so a little faster than the last.

And it ate. That was the one fact the House never wrote in its books. It ate every generation's answer and it was never full — not fuller, not slower, not sleeping. Owen's foundation stone. Turretin's fine seam. All of it went down the same throat and the throat was exactly as empty afterward.

That is what it was, you see. Not a debt, which can be settled. Not a wound, which can close. An *appetite*. And there is no quantity of anything that satisfies an appetite. There is only the next meal.

## IX

Packer was the last of them.

He was not a lesser wizard than the others. He may have been the honest one. He went down with a lamp like all of them, and he stood in the bedrock dark and looked at the thing, and he did what none of them had done: he counted.

He counted the moves. Out of the Beast, into honour, into justice, into essence, split once, split again, sealed under a fine name. Seventeen centuries of carrying one box from room to room, and it was heavier now than the day it came in on Anselm's hem.

And he understood that there was no room left in the House to put it, and no wizard coming after him with a better one.

So he made his choice, and I will not pretend it was cowardice. He climbed back up, and he took the lamp with him, and he hung a curtain across the stair.

"It is necessary," he told them. "It must not be looked into. It is a mystery. Let no one carry a light below this line."

And the House believed him, because they were tired.

And a hunger that must be fed and may not be *seen* is the oldest doom in any world — because you cannot measure what you cannot see, and so you can never know how much it wants, and so the only safe amount is *everything*.

It took the foundations first, and it was patient. Then the floors. Then the great hall, and the library, and the towers where the wizards had done their fine work, and the road, and the gate where the traveller had stood in the rain. It took the villages. It took the hill. It ate the light out of the sky, and then it ate the sky, and then there was nothing at all in any direction, and no one left to be hungry for.

And that was the end of the world, and it had happened exactly as designed, and every step of it had been taken by a good man solving a real problem.

## X

In the nothing, someone was standing.

He had scars on his hands and he was not surprised.

He looked at the place where the House had been, and at the empty dark where the Beast had once held its deeds, and at the long ruin of seventeen centuries of careful, faithful, wrong work — and he did not condemn any of it. He knew their names. He had loved every one of them, including Owen, who had gone deepest and paid most.

Then he reached out and took the hour by its edge, the way you'd take a page, and turned it back.

The dark ran backwards. The towers unbuilt themselves; the curtain unhung; the fine seam unstitched and the foundation stone came unmortared and the great weight lifted out of the bedrock and went back up the stair. The cart unbuilt. The rule of honour unwritten. And the small thing that had come in on a hem went back out the door, and out of the hall, and down the steps of the House, and into the dark under the roots where hungry things belong — and the door of the House stood open on an evening seventeen centuries gone, and it was clean, and nothing inside it wanted anything.

## XI

The physician was sitting on the step in the last of the light.

The plague was ending. He was tired the way you are tired after good work.

Someone sat down beside him, and they were quiet a while, and then the physician said, because it had been on his mind all evening:

"I told them death follows a turning-away. I keep my word." He turned his hands over. "But I have been sitting here wondering how they'll hear it. Whether they'll think the word is a *chain* on me. Whether they'll go looking for what's holding my hand."

"They will," said the one beside him. "And they'll find something down there, and they'll bring it up into the house, and they'll spend seventeen hundred years trying to find a room for it."

The physician was silent for a long moment.

"Then let me say it better," he said.

"Yes," said the other. "That's why I came."

And he leaned in, and he said the thing the whole world had been built wrong for want of, and it was very short:

*Nothing is holding my hand. I am not hungry. I never was. I keep my word because I love them — and death is the sickness, not the sentence, and I have come to cure it, and there is nobody to pay.*

The physician wept, and then he laughed, and then he got up and went back inside to the dying, and the sun went down on a world with no monster in it at all.
