# The Necessity Problem

### How penal substitution keeps moving its central difficulty instead of solving it

---

## What this is

This came out of working through the atonement-theories map — reading each position's problem, its move, and the new problem it opens, and following the arrows rather than the commentary.

The thesis being tested was simple: penal substitutionary atonement (PSA) doesn't resolve its central difficulty. It takes retributive justice apart and redistributes the pieces so that no single place in the system ever displays the whole contradiction at once. Every generation moves a piece, relieves the pressure where it was showing, and creates pressure somewhere else.

That turned out to be right, and more specific than expected. There are really two things being shuffled — a *currency* (the stuff that changes hands) and a set of *deontic properties* (obligations, desert, necessity). The first half of this document maps both. The second half follows just one thread — the question of what makes the cross necessary — because that thread turns out to carry the whole argument.

---

## Five words that do the heavy lifting

**Retributive justice.** Punishment deserved because of what was done, proportioned to the offence, owed to the offender. It looks backward at the crime. Contrast **restorative justice**, which looks forward and aims at repairing the person and the relationship.

**Satisfaction.** Something offered to an offended party that makes it acceptable for them not to exact the penalty. The whole Western tradition after Anselm runs on this word.

**Deontic.** Anything in the "ought" family — duty, obligation, permission, desert. A *deontic property* is something like guilt, merit, debt, or righteousness: not a physical thing, but treated in these systems as though it can be owned, owed, transferred, or cancelled. When this document says PSA trades in deontic currency, it means the stuff moving between God and man isn't people or objects, it's abstract moral standing.

**Absolute vs. hypothetical necessity.** Absolute necessity means it could not have been otherwise, full stop. Hypothetical necessity (also called consequent or conditional necessity) means it's necessary *given some earlier free choice* — once you've made a promise you must keep it, but you needn't have promised. This distinction is the hinge of the entire argument, and the tradition oscillates between the two without ever settling.

**Pure act** (Latin *actus purus*). The classical claim that God has no unrealised potential — nothing in him is waiting to happen, nothing in him can be switched on later. It travels with **divine simplicity** (God isn't assembled out of parts; his attributes aren't components, they're identical with what he is) and **immutability** (God doesn't change). Hold these three together and you have the wall that the whole tradition eventually runs into.

A fuller glossary is at the end.

## Part one — the apparatus

### Retributive justice isn't one thing

If you're going to claim that a system takes retributive justice apart and hides the pieces, you have to say what the pieces are. Minimally, retribution needs five things:

1. **Desert** — guilt attaches to a particular person and merits a penalty.
2. **The guilt–penalty tie** — the penalty must land on the one who deserves it. This is what makes it *retribution* rather than just harm.
3. **Proportion** — the penalty is matched to the offence. Too little and it's a hand-slap; too much and it's cruelty.
4. **Necessity** — it genuinely must be exacted. Something makes it unavoidable.
5. **Discharge** — once paid, the matter is closed.

Two more things are needed to run any of this as a *transaction*, so they get their own rows:

6. **Currency** — what actually changes hands.
7. **Transfer device** — the mechanism by which it moves.

Track those seven across the history and you can watch the machine get built, strained, and abandoned.

### Reading the tables

- `·` — not present in this system at all
- plain text — carried over unchanged from the position to its left
- **bold** — invented, moved, or redefined here
- `A / B` — the item has been *bisected* into two halves
- `✕` — dropped, broken, or under direct attack
- *italic* — the seed: present before anyone had a use for it

Note the column order is derivational rather than chronological — Origen actually predates Athanasius by a century. Athanasius sits first because he functions as the baseline: what the system looks like with almost none of this machinery installed.

Four things jump out.

**Six of the seven slots start empty.** That reframes the whole exercise. These aren't features the tradition inherited from scripture and then organised — they're apparatus it built, and you can date each piece.

**The two layers have different birthdays.** Origen starts the currency layer: exchange, a liable party, a transfer device, automatic discharge. But both specifically *retributive* slots stay empty — no guilt–penalty tie, no proportion. Ransom is a transaction without retribution. Retribution proper doesn't arrive until Anselm, eight centuries later. So the "currency" half and the "deontic" half of PSA aren't two aspects of one idea; they're two separate inventions, and one is much older than the other.

**Automatic discharge is a fossil.** Origen introduces it for an obvious reason — a purchase frees the captive. It then rides along untouched through Anselm, Calvin, and Owen's *Death of Death* before Owen has to kill it in 1655. Which means the double-payment argument for limited atonement — the signature Reformed doctrine — rests on an assumption inherited from the ransom theory that Anselm wrote *Cur Deus Homo* to destroy.

**Anselm doesn't fail to supply a transfer device; he breaks one.** Moving the creditor from Satan to God voids the purchase model — you can't buy something back from its own owner. The slot then sits empty for 450 years until Calvin fills it with imputation. That's why the cell is marked destroyed rather than absent, and it explains the complaint that satisfaction theory never says how anything reaches the believer.

### Socinus, 1578

Everything after this point is written under pressure from one man, so he gets his own paragraph rather than a column.

Faustus Socinus pressed two blades at once. First: if satisfaction is genuinely paid in full, the debt is *discharged*, not forgiven — so grace has vanished and God is simply collecting. Second: guilt is the most personal thing a person has, and cannot justly be signed over to an innocent substitute.

He was heterodox on a great deal else, and it doesn't matter. The objection is clean, and every subsequent move in the penal tradition is a response to it. Grotius and Kant left the system rather than answer it. Owen and Turretin stayed and went looking for somewhere to put the retribution where the objection couldn't reach.

Two rows are worth staring at.

**Desert never moves.** From Origen to Packer, seventeen centuries, the liability sits on man and nobody ever proposes otherwise. Everything else gets relocated, subdivided, or abandoned. That's the anchor — and the fact that it's the *only* fixed point tells you something about what the system is actually for.

**The transfer-device row rhymes at both ends.** Athanasius has union. Owen in 1677 lands on union. When double imputation gets accused of being a legal fiction — a verdict that declares you righteous without making you righteous — Owen's answer is to ground it in union with Christ. The terminal repair of the forensic system is borrowed from the ontological system it was built to replace.

## Part two — the necessity thread

Of the seven rows, one turns out to drive all the others.

### The question it's answering

Every cell in the necessity row answers the same question: **given that God wants to save, what stops him from simply saving?**

Not "why did Christ die," but "what is the obstacle, and where is it kept." Everything else in the system is machinery for satisfying whatever the obstacle turns out to be. Change the obstacle's address and the machinery has to be rebuilt — which is why necessity moves first and the other rows move afterward.

### The trade-off that governs it

There's a single axis running underneath the whole row:

> The further **out** the constraint sits, the safer God's nature is — and the more it looks like an insult to his sovereignty.
> The further **in** it sits, the more sovereign God looks — and the more the constraint damages his nature.

And the pressure is one-directional. Objections from sovereignty — *nothing outside God may bind God* — arrive constantly and always push inward. Objections from the doctrine of God — simplicity, immutability, pure act — only bite once the constraint is already deep inside. So for fifteen hundred years there's a strong wind blowing one way and no wind at all blowing back, until suddenly there's a wall.

### The three regimes

The regime column names what *kind* of operation is being performed, and the phases turn out to be strictly ordered.

**Translation** — the constraint is moved from one container to another. The devil's rights, then God's honour, then his justice, then his will, then a covenant, then his essence.

**Fission** — once the constraint reaches the divine essence there is nowhere further in to go, so the motion changes kind. The container itself starts splitting. Owen divides necessity into fact and mode; Turretin divides the attribute into disposition and exercise.

**Evaporation** — when division stops helping, the locus is emptied. Packer keeps the claim that satisfaction was necessary and declines to say what makes it so.

Reading down the column: two origins, five translations, two fissions, one evaporation. No fission happens before translation is exhausted; no translation happens after fission begins. That's not what a tradition wandering around looks like. It's what a search space being consumed in order looks like.

**Owen is the hinge, personally.** He performs the last translation in 1653 and the first fission in 1655 — two years apart, against the same opponent. The phase boundary of the entire tradition sits inside one man's working life. He didn't discover that necessity belonged in the divine essence and then discover it needed subdividing. He ran out of room and changed operations.

One more regularity worth noting: **the necessity slot only goes slack when something else is temporarily carrying the weight.** Owen 1647 is the single retrograde move — necessity drifts back *out* to the divine will — and it happens precisely when the double-payment argument is doing the work instead. The moment Socinus knocks that prop out, necessity tightens again and keeps tightening.

### The two walls

Here's why the search couldn't succeed, and it's worse than one problem.

**Wall one — the doctrine of God.** Push the necessity inward to protect God's sovereignty and you eventually reach his essence. But retributive justice is existentially dependent on guilt: take away guilt and it has nothing to do. So before the fall, this supposedly essential attribute has no object. An essential attribute with nothing to act on is an unrealised capacity — a potency — in a being who is supposed to be pure act. And when the creature sins and the capacity switches on, that's a change in God. Put bluntly: on this account, we broke God when we sinned.

**Wall two — the doctrine of the Trinity.** This one is less often noticed, and it follows from the same move. Once the obligation is *internal* to God, the only parties left to transact are the divine persons. Hence the pre-temporal pact, hence eternal suretyship, hence Christ standing before the Father. But a transaction between co-equals is either vacuous — nothing can really change hands between persons who share one essence — or it is subordinating, because a genuine payment requires one party to gain what the other gives up. You can have an intelligible transaction or you can have trinitarian equality. Not both.

The framers were not sloppy. They were working hard to preserve exactly these commitments, and the moves are ingenious. But the two walls close in as the constraint moves inward, and every remaining move trades one for the other. Turretin buys sovereignty and pays in pure act. The covenant buys necessity and pays in trinitarian equality. Owen 1677 buys reality for imputation and pays by borrowing from the system PSA replaced. Packer buys peace and pays with the entire rational apparatus.

## Part three — the exits

Everyone who leaves PSA leaves by the same door. Whatever else divides them, every departure is an operation on the necessity slot:

| Position | Action | What becomes of the necessity |
|---|---|---|
| **Grotius**, 1617 | relaxation | Moves out of God and into the requirements of government. God is Ruler rather than creditor, and the penalty becomes relaxable. |
| **The New Divinity**, 18th–19th c. | dissolution | Redefined as benevolent rectitude — God's disposition to do what best serves the moral system. No retributive necessity remains to house. |
| **Socinus, Kant** | denial | Never granted in the first place. Nothing to locate. |
| **Campbell, Torrance** | evacuation | The slot is returned to empty. This is the Athanasian baseline restored — minus the divine dilemma that put the seed there. |

That's the useful way to read the non-penal half of the map. These look like four different theories with four different concerns — a jurist, a school of New England Calvinists, a Socinian and a Kantian, two Scots working from the Greek fathers. Structurally they are one move made four times: **stop trying to find somewhere to put the necessity.**

Which also explains why the arguments between them and PSA never resolve. They aren't offering rival answers to the question "where does the necessity live." They're refusing the question.

**Packer is not a fifth exit.** It's worth separating him out, because his operation looks similar and isn't. The four exits above remove the *claim* — there's no necessity left to house, so having no location for it is perfectly coherent. Packer removes the *location* while keeping the claim. He still asserts that satisfaction was necessary; he simply declines to say what makes it so, on the grounds that the model is a disclosure rather than a blueprint. That leaves the system in a worse formal state than doing nothing would have, and it's why the move reads as surrender rather than as a genuine alternative.

There's a further awkwardness. Every position on the penal spine was built by classical logic — each one framed as a correction of the last, argued on the premises of the previous move. Declaring the resulting doctrine exempt from logical inspection disowns the method that produced it. And the exemption is not extended to rival models: the same year Packer asks for latitude on penal substitution, he's pressing Dodd hard on reducing propitiation to expiation.

## The root objection

Stripped to one paragraph:

Penal substitution requires a necessity that is simultaneously strong enough to make the cross unavoidable and weak enough to leave God's acceptance of a substitute gracious. No single location can support both. So the necessity gets moved — outward for sovereignty's sake, inward for necessity's sake — until it arrives in the divine essence, where it becomes an unrealised potential in a being who is supposed to have none, and drags the transaction inside the Trinity, where a payment between co-equals is either meaningless or subordinating. Every subsequent move is a subdivision of that same problem, and the last move is to declare it a mystery.

The conclusion isn't that the retribution was hidden in the wrong place. It's that a good and infinite God has no use for retribution, so there is no right place. Remove the contingent necessity and there is nothing left to relocate, split, or conceal — and you are back at Athanasius, minus the dilemma.

## Glossary

**Aseity** — God's existing from himself, dependent on nothing outside himself.

**Deontic** — belonging to the "ought" family: duty, obligation, permission, desert. A deontic property is abstract moral standing treated as though it can be owned or transferred.

**Discharge** — the closing of an account once payment is made. In a money model this is automatic; the point of Owen's 1655 penal/pecuniary distinction is to deny that it is.

**Forensic** — courtroom language. A forensic change is a change in legal standing, a verdict — as opposed to an ontological change, which is a change in what something actually is.

**Habitus / actus** — a disposition versus its exercise. Being a pianist versus playing the piano. Turretin's key distinction.

**Hypothetical (consequent, conditional) necessity** — necessary *given* some prior free choice, as a promise binds only once made. Contrast **absolute necessity**, which could not have been otherwise at all.

**Immutability** — that God does not change.

**Imputation** — crediting something to someone's account that isn't inherently theirs. Double imputation: the believer's sin reckoned to Christ, Christ's righteousness reckoned to the believer.

**Ontological** — having to do with being. An ontological account of salvation says human nature is actually healed, not merely re-classified.

***Pactum salutis*** (covenant of redemption) — a pre-creation agreement among the persons of the Trinity concerning salvation, with the Son standing as surety for the elect. Systematised in the mid-seventeenth century, and load-bearing for Owen.

**Pecuniary vs. penal** — a money debt versus a punishment. Owen's 1655 distinction: money debts discharge automatically on payment, punishments don't, so grace survives.

**Potency / potentiality** — a capacity to become something you aren't yet. Classical theism denies any of this in God.

**Pure act** (*actus purus*) — God has no unrealised potential.

**Rectoral justice** — justice belonging to a ruler governing a system, concerned with the good order of the whole rather than with any individual's desert. Grotius's central category.

**Simplicity** — God is not composed of parts; his attributes are not components but identical with his essence.

**Suretyship** — standing as guarantor for another's debt.

**Vindicatory justice** — Owen's term for God's justice as it punishes sin; retributive justice under another name.

## Where this is contestable

Worth flagging honestly, because these are the places a critic will push.

**The Athanasius column is interpretively loaded.** Marking desert as absent is a reading, not a report. And the necessity cell — God's truthfulness binding him to the sentence of death — is precisely the *inconsistency* in Athanasius rather than a settled position of his. It sits beside his main line, that death is the natural consequence of turning from the source of life. Calling it "the seed" is an argument, not an observation, and it needs to be presented as one.

**Placing the federal covenant as a discrete column oversimplifies.** The covenant of redemption has several parents — Olevianus and Ursinus upstream, Dickson, Cocceius as its great systematiser, Owen developing it himself. Owen was already working within a federal framework in 1647. The column marks when the framework gets systematised, not when the ideas first appear, and it's labelled by framework rather than by person to avoid a priority dispute that adds nothing.

**Column order is derivational, not chronological.** Origen predates Athanasius. The ordering follows the argument's dependencies, which is the more useful arrangement here, but it should be stated rather than quietly assumed.

**"Every exit is the same move" is a strong claim.** It's defensible on the necessity row specifically. It would be overreach to say Grotius, Kant, and Torrance are doing the same thing in any broader sense — they differ profoundly on what the cross accomplishes, on the person of Christ, and on almost everything else. The claim is narrow: on the single question of where necessity is housed, all four decline to answer, and that's why none of them can be argued with on PSA's own terms.
