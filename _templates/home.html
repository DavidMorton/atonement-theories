To see Jesus is to see God

{{ site.title }}

If you grew up believing God loves you but could never quite shake the feeling that he's holding something back, you're in the right place. That unease isn't weak faith; it's a reasonable response to a picture of God who cannot forgive until someone is punished. There is no God hiding behind Jesus, and this site is my attempt to show you where that hidden God came from.

The theory you're likely bothered by is called penal substitutionary atonement, and it claims God is unable to simply forgive. Not unwilling, but unable. That inability is about our guilt, and our guilt did not exist until we made it. This is a study of what seventeen centuries of careful people did with that problem.

The argument in brief

Penal substitutionary atonement requires a constraint on God that did not exist before the fall — a necessity God acquires when the creature sins. Before there is guilt, nothing obliges God to punish. After there is guilt, he cannot do otherwise.

And it has nowhere to sit. Housed outside God, it insults his sovereignty: something that is not God now binds God. Housed inside him, it damages his nature: an attribute that is essential yet has no object until a creature sins is an unrealised capacity in a being who is supposed to have none.

So the constraint migrates, and it migrates one way. Every move operates on the modality of the constraint — whether the “must” is absolute or hypothetical, whether it sits in the will or the essence — while leaving its object untouched. In every location the object is creaturely. The defect was always in the object.

If this feels like it’s pulling a thread you can’t afford to lose

Rejecting penal substitution can feel like abandoning your faith. It doesn’t have to be. Start with Athanasius’ On the Incarnation — the ontological account is the soft landing for anyone who has only ever heard the gospel in a penal frame. This project exists because it’s what let me keep my faith, not lose it.
